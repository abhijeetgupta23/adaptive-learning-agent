# What Video Games Have to Teach Us About Learning and Literacy

Citation: Gee, J. P. (2003). *What Video Games Have to Teach Us About Learning and Literacy.* Palgrave Macmillan. (36 learning principles distilled from commercial video games.)

Gee enumerates 36 principles. Ten of them carry the most weight for designing short, generative, single-session learning games:

1. **Active, Critical Learning.** The game environment is designed so the player must form, test, and revise hypotheses — not consume content. Mechanic implication: every screen demands a player decision; nothing autoplays.

2. **Just-in-Time and On-Demand.** Information arrives at the moment of need, in the form the action requires. Rules and constraints are surfaced when the player encounters them, not in a pre-game manual.

3. **Achievement (Calibrated Rewards).** Rewards are tuned to current ability — they signal real progress, not participation. Bad games over-reward; good games escalate the reward signal as challenge rises.

4. **Performance before Competence.** Players are allowed to *act* with a system before they fully understand it; mastery is built through doing. This is the inverse of "lecture first, practice second" and is the foundation of game-based scaffolding.

5. **Identity.** Effective games offer the player a role to inhabit — detective, builder, doctor — that aligns with the cognitive operation being learned. Identity carries motivation cheaply.

6. **Risk-Taking.** Failure cost is bounded and recoverable: try, fail, retry. This unlocks exploratory cognition that high-stakes single-shot quizzes suppress.

7. **Well-Ordered Problems.** Early problems are chosen so the strategies they teach generalize well to harder problems. The first puzzle isn't easy — it's *generative*.

8. **Practice in a Pleasurable Context.** Repetition is repackaged as variation: same skill, new surface. The boredom ceiling of practice rises sharply when the surface changes.

9. **Situated Meanings.** Words and symbols get their meaning from the game-world contexts they appear in — definitions are constructed from use, not memorized in isolation.

10. **Distributed Knowledge.** Cognition is shared between player, tools, and the environment. The interface holds state the player doesn't have to; the player holds the strategy the interface doesn't encode.

For a generation system: these ten principles are the *targets* a Game Agent should be able to cite when defending its mechanic choice. They are the operational form of "why this game, for this concept."
