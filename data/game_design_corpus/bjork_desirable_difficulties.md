# Making Things Hard on Yourself, But in a Good Way: Desirable Difficulties

Citation: Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2011). *Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way: Creating desirable difficulties to enhance learning.* In M. A. Gernsbacher et al. (Eds.), Psychology and the real world (pp. 56–64).

Bjork & Bjork's central counter-intuition is that the conditions that maximize *current* performance are typically the conditions that minimize *durable* learning. Smooth, fluent, immediate-success practice produces a feeling of mastery that does not transfer. Effortful, error-permitting practice feels worse in the moment and produces transfer that lasts. Five techniques are designated as **desirable difficulties**:

**Spacing.** Distribute practice over time rather than massing it. The forgetting that happens between sessions is exactly what retrieval re-strengthens. Implication for a single-session learning game: build *within-session* spacing by varying intermediate tasks before returning to the target concept, rather than 20 reps in a row.

**Interleaving.** Mix problems of different types within a practice block rather than blocking them by type. Interleaving forces the learner to *select* a strategy on every problem rather than just *applying* a pre-selected one. Implication: game rounds shouldn't all use the same surface — mix the variant types.

**Variability.** Train under varied conditions, surfaces, and contexts. Skills trained narrowly transfer narrowly. Implication: a join-classification game should rotate domains (orders/customers, employees/departments, books/authors) within a single session.

**Generation Effect.** Asking the learner to produce an answer — even if they fail — strengthens memory more than passively reading the answer. Implication: never reveal before the player commits.

**Retrieval Practice.** Quizzing is a learning event, not just an assessment event. The act of pulling information out of memory strengthens the memory trace far more than re-reading. Implication: the game's win condition should be a retrieval, not a recognition.

The framework gives a generation system a clean predictor: any game whose feedback comes *before* the player has committed an answer, or whose rounds are all the same surface, or whose difficulty curve is monotonically smooth, is producing fluency without learning. The Game Agent should bake in mild, calibrated difficulty: plausible distractors, short delays before reveal, and small surface variation across rounds.
