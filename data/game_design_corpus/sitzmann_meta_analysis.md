# A Meta-Analytic Examination of the Instructional Effectiveness of Computer-Based Simulation Games

Citation: Sitzmann, T. (2011). *A meta-analytic examination of the instructional effectiveness of computer-based simulation games.* Personnel Psychology, 64(2), 489–528.

Sitzmann's meta-analysis (k=65 studies, N≈6,500 trainees) is the most-cited quantitative evidence for game-based learning effect sizes. The headline numbers:

- **Declarative knowledge:** trainees in simulation games scored **+11%** higher than trainees in comparison conditions.
- **Procedural knowledge:** **+14%** higher.
- **Retention** (delayed post-tests): **+9%** higher.
- **Self-efficacy:** **+20%** higher.

These are real, replicable effects — but the paper's deeper contribution is the **boundary conditions**: when games work, and when they don't.

**Games work when:**
- Trainees actively interact with the material (mechanics force decisions; passive cutscenes don't count).
- The game is *supplemental* to other instruction, not a replacement. Games layered on top of conventional content beat conventional content alone by the largest margins.
- Trainees can access the game on demand and play repeatedly. Multi-session, learner-paced access produces the biggest gains.

**Games fail (or underperform comparison conditions) when:**
- The game is the *only* instructional medium and replaces previously-effective active instruction. Replacing a good worked-example sequence with a mediocre game is a net loss.
- Material is passive — narrated cutscenes, video-only "games," or quiz-disguised-as-game formats produce no advantage.
- Single-use mandatory access prevents the spacing and re-engagement that drive retention gains.

**Publication bias caveat.** Sitzmann's trim-and-fill analysis suggests effect sizes are likely **inflated by ~20-30%** by selective reporting; the true effects are real but smaller than the headline numbers. Recent multi-level meta-analyses (Xu & Dai 2026; Wouters et al. 2013) converge on similar moderated-to-large effects with the same boundary conditions.

For a generation system: Sitzmann's findings are a license to ship games as **the active layer on top of** worked examples, retrieval prompts, and explicit instruction — not as a replacement. The system's defaulting to Game with Worked-Example as silent fallback is consistent with the meta-analytic pattern: games dominate when concepts admit interaction; worked examples cover the residue.
